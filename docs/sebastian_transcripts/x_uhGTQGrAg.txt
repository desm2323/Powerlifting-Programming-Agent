Today I'm going to be talking about training splits. [... critiques push/pull/legs
— prefers to superset push and pull to get twice the work in the same time,
except when very strong or close to a comp and you want a standalone heavy bench
...]

The first thing we have to understand is availability to the individual. If I'm
onboarding anybody the first question is: how many days do you have available to
allocate to your training? That dictates the trajectory of the entire program.
What I consider optimal for maximizing strength and muscle building is four to
five days. [...] The greater the frequency the lower the intensity. And the flip
side is also true: the lower the frequency the greater the intensity should be.

[... research on 1x vs 2x vs 3x/wk per body part: when volume is equated there's
very little difference; practically, two times per week per body part is the
recommendation. Studies are short (~8 weeks). ...]

Optimal training volume: roughly 10 to 20 sets per body part per week for
hypertrophy — mutually agreed by the authorities. Start at the lowest end of that
range and gradually increase if you plateau. For strength it's lower — anywhere
between 6 to 12 total sets per movement pattern per week (Dr. Rhea and colleagues,
~2009). When we talk hypertrophy we think building quads, glutes, lats, pecs (per
muscle group). When we talk strength we think building a squat pattern, a bench
pattern, a pull pattern (per movement pattern). Big difference. When calculating
total volume I think of volume load = reps x sets x load lifted. The stronger the
individual, the longer rest between sets and between sessions.

If you're training two times per week I highly recommend taking your exercises to
a close proximity to failure. Quite often you might hear me talk about periodizing
effort where we start our week one of any of our programs with conservative loads
to allow room to progress from week to week. Now, this also allows room for
recovery for the remainder of the week. I talk about treating it like a marathon
where we pace ourselves at the start. But when it comes to two days per week, that
is a sprint — you got 5 days to recover, so send it. On the flip side, the higher
the frequency, the lower the intensity should be.

[... 2-day split: do WHOLE BODY (push/pull/squat/hinge) not upper/lower, because
saturating one half makes the back half junk volume; use different patterns each
of the two sessions. ...]

[... 3-day splits: whole-body each session works well except for highly elite /
high-absolute-strength athletes (warm-ups take too long). The Lilybridge split:
squat+deadlift on one day (heavy squat + light deadlift one week, reverse the
next), a heavy bench day, and a whole-body day — works because heavy spine loading
happens only once per week. PPL over three days: not enough frequency for
quadriceps or upper-body push. DUP (squat/bench/deadlift every day, heavy/
moderate/light): great for skill, too much for elite high-absolute-strength
lifters, and needs added upper-back work. Strength System 3-day: squat day /
upper body / deadlift day with quad accessory (e.g. leg extensions) for a second
quad exposure. ...]

[... 4-day split (Sebastian's preferred, "five gold stars"): upper horizontal
focus / lower quad focus / upper vertical focus / lower posterior focus —
the most balanced. Female variation: one full upper + one whole-body day for less
upper-body volume. ...]

Something I like to consider with 5 days of frequency: there is data — not the
best research, but I agree with it — that your muscles stop growing after about 5
days of recovery, at which point they start shrinking.

[... 5-day splits are the upper end of recommendations; can be too much for high-
absolute-strength athletes but optimal for lightweight high-relative-strength
athletes. Discusses absolute vs relative strength (Thor Bjornson ~200kg vs Stefi
Cohen <60kg): the greater the absolute strength, the greater the demand for
recovery — rest between sets and sessions must increase. Charles Poliquin 5-day
rotation (squat/upper/deadlift alternating). ...]

In my opinion, squats need less time to recover than deadlifts. So to give them
equal priority (5 days a week for squats and 5 for deadlift, as Poliquin's split
implies) is a bit of an imbalance. My sweet spot is two times a week for upper
body, two times a week for squats, one time per week for deadlifts. So my 5-day
split is: squat twice, upper body twice, deadlift once.

Here's a bit of the science (backed by practical application): heavy spine loading
multiple times per week isn't happening with high-absolute-strength athletes —
enter the Lilybridge method where they squat and deadlift on the same day because
the spine can only handle one heavy session per week. And training morale matters:
squatters love squatting and hate deadlifting (and vice versa); you bring higher
morale and effort to the lifts you love, which biases performance.

[... 6-day split: reserved for people who need to be in the gym daily (e.g. mental
health); push/pull/legs x2, with the deadlift on the lower-body day, NOT the pull
day. Not considered superior — same volume spread over six days. ...]

So there is today's session on training splits. Every good coach needs to know how
to write great programs, and understanding all the different splits available is a
huge component of program design. I hope you found it valuable. See you next time.
