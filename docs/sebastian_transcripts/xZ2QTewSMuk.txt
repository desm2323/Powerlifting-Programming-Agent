Periodization styles for performance versus aesthetics. Typically, the discussion
revolves around periodization strategies for hypertrophy versus strength.
However, you are going to see why I've made that differentiation, and this
presentation is geared towards industry professionals. This is my mentorship
group that I'm presenting in front of. So, they have a base level of knowledge.
However, I like to simplify these concepts so that anyone understands it. So, you
don't need a degree. You don't need to be an industry professional. You do love
to know how to train and love to know what programming involves and periodization
and all of those things, and I'm going to teach you more of that. Something that I
always learned was the smarter I got, the stronger I got. So, that's how I like to
treat all the people that I work with is teach them about the concepts that taught
me how to be really, really strong so that you guys can also be really, really
strong. Anyway, let's get into it. Today is one of my favorite topics, and that's
periodization. But, it's going to be framed in a slightly different way. What I'm
talking about today is periodization for performance versus aesthetics. It's
usually framed as periodization for strength versus hypertrophy. However, I
believe that's the wrong framing. Why? Because I would say strength is needed for
hypertrophy, and hypertrophy is needed for strength. So, to say there's a specific
style of periodization for strength, it's like, well, strength is one of the
phases that we do for performance over the long-term plan, just as is hypertrophy.
And vice versa is also true. So, instead of calling them strength versus
hypertrophy, I'm calling them for what they are, performance and aesthetics.
[... reframes performance (powerlifting, weightlifting, strongman, CrossFit, plus
non-strength sports) vs aesthetics (hypertrophy via training + body composition
via nutrition) ...]

First, we have to understand, and then I like to define all of these terms.
Periodization, the formal definition: the manipulation and logical sequencing of
training variables over the long term to reach a certain goal by a specific date
or competition. The problem with this is if someone asks you what periodization
is and you spit this out, it doesn't really tell you much. So, basically,
periodization and programming isn't the same thing. Program sits within the
periodized plan. The periodized plan is your long-term plan. It's how we split up
these different training variables into specific phases and organize them in a
logical sequence so that we can peak when we need to peak, such as a competition
date.

The analogy that I like to use is the man who chases two rabbits doesn't eat. What
do those rabbits represent? The rabbits represent our training modalities,
hypertrophy, strength endurance, strength, power. A lot of the times if you ask
someone, "What do you want to achieve over the long term?" It's like, "I want all
of that, thanks." Now, the problem is when we provide equal emphasis to all of
those physical qualities, we end up not really achieving high-levels in any of
them. Science and practical experience show trying to develop multiple qualities
at the same time with equal emphasis produces diluted outcomes. The strategies
where you do train multiple physical qualities simultaneously successfully will
always prioritize a main physical quality and utilize different physical
qualities on a maintenance level. Okay? So, you apply a lot more volume and focus
on the main physical quality. And with your accessory work, you maintain the
other physical qualities that you don't want to detrain.

Now, in general, these long-term plans — there's different styles of
periodization such as conjugate, undulating, linear (a.k.a. traditional), block
periodization. If someone asked me what style of periodization to use, I kind of
have to stop and think. It's like, well, I guess I use all of it. You can and
should integrate different styles of periodization. There's linearity components
to all of our programming. We undulate — undulate comes from the Latin word unda,
which means wave. So we're moving up and down: accumulation (high volume),
intensification (low volume), back to accumulation. A more linear approach is high
volume, less volume, less volume, peak, with intensity inversely proportionate.
The general premise is to begin the long-term plan with high volume and greater
training variation to build a broad base. A pyramid will only peak as high as the
base is wide. Over time volume is gradually reduced so intensity can increase, and
exercises become less varied and more specific, allowing the athlete to reach peak
performance by the time of competition.

Now, I'm going to talk in plain English. My five-step model of periodization,
based on Tudor Bompa's model but with different terminology, assuming the
individual is starting from scratch:

Learn to move first. No matter what you do, you need to understand the lifting
techniques on the fundamental movement patterns: a squat, a hinge, a push, and a
pull. One exercise from each category, do it all in one day, two to three days
per week. Minimize the training variation, emphasis on movement literacy. The
higher the complexity (squat, deadlift) the lower the reps to avoid technical
breakdown; lower complexity (push, pull) can use higher reps. It doesn't take
long — two to four weeks. You don't need muscle soreness on your first day.

Build work capacity. Now we go to adding a little bit more volume, more effort,
working a little bit harder, teaching the individual what it feels like to work
hard gradually. Here's my favorite strategy: add an extra set every week. Week one
two sets, next week three sets, next week four sets, next week five sets. We've
accumulated a ton of volume and gradually built work capacity while working at a
higher RPE every session.

Hypertrophy. This is the highest volume phase of our long-term plan. How much
volume is appropriate? A lot of the research tells us 10 to 20 sets per body part
per week is a rough guideline. You don't need to start at the high end. I like to
start at the minimal effective dose and work up if we need to. Most cases, you
don't need to. Now — and this is important — typically, we're increasing the
volume, not via increasing reps and sets, but rather increasing the load that
we're lifting. Okay? So, 10 sets of 10 on 100 kg. Then we add 5 kg or 5%. So, 10
by 10 on 105 kg. The next week, 110 kg. So by simply increasing the loads that
we're lifting from week to week, which is what we should be doing, we are
increasing volume without having to manipulate the reps and sets. So don't always
default to: as we build more work capacity, we have to do more reps and sets to be
effective sessions. No, not necessarily. I very rarely perform the top end of that
range, 20 sets per muscle group per week. Typically I'm at the lower end, 8 to 12
for the majority of my programs. We do the appropriate amount of work for that
individual for that day — the stronger you are, the longer your rest between sets,
the less total sets you're going to be doing in that day, but you're doing a lot
heavier weight. Four to five days per week with a hypertrophy phase is the sweet
spot. There's also research suggesting we can achieve meaningful gains with a lot
less volume — even one banger of a top set per muscle group or movement pattern,
so long as that movement is taken absolutely to fail.

Research-backed insight on the hypertrophy phase: a meta-analysis by Brad
Schoenfeld and colleagues found that hypertrophy can be achieved with a broad
range of reps, anywhere between 5 to 30, as long as the movement was taken to the
same proximity to failure. That doesn't mean choose whatever rep range you want —
consider the complexity of the movement. The greater the complexity, the lower the
reps. The muscle that you're targeting needs to be the limiting factor of the
movement. Mechanical tension is key — focus on intensity over absolute load.
Training like a bodybuilder, you don't need to lift heavy weights. This is where
people get the illusion that bodybuilders are weak — they just haven't practiced
the skill of lifting heavy weights, but with large amounts of muscle mass they
have huge strength potential.

Next phase, strength. Reduce the reps, so now load does matter. A lot of the
research points to about 80% and plus. 80% of your one rep max — what's the
appropriate rep range? Six reps and less, to put it simple. What's the secondary
exercise after our squats on a strength phase? Do we still need to do 80%? The
answer is no. Most people are using conjugate style of periodization, where the
first exercise of the day is strength-rep-range-specific to achieve a strength
outcome, and the secondary/accessory exercises support that with a hypertrophy
stimulus — you don't have to do your bicep and tricep work with 80% of your max.
The volume is slightly less than hypertrophy: research points to about 6 to 12
total sets per movement pattern per week (Dr. Rhea and colleagues), three to four
sets per movement pattern per session, two to three times per week. When it's
hypertrophy, it's per muscle group per week. When it's strength, it's per movement
pattern per week. Very big difference.

Peaking phase. The objective: maximize performance by being as specific as
possible, and minimizing the volume by taking out everything else that is a
variation of what you want to do. All of your bicep curls, tricep extensions, calf
raises may not be necessary — the first place to look when reducing volume is the
exercises that are not specific. So this is the phase where a lot of people say
"we're deloading, we're getting close to comp, so we have to go lighter." No — we
reduce the fatigue, which is usually accumulated by the accumulation of volume. We
reduce the volume rather than reducing the intensity. We need to maintain the
intensity. If you were to step into a competition, you need to be working really,
really hard. The sprinters in their peaking phase are running really fast — this
is the fastest they'll be running in their entire long-term plan — they're doing
way less volume, but the intensity needs to be at the highest, specific to
competition. If you want to lift heavy in your sport, you've got to lift heavy in
your peaking phase.

How do we modify this for aesthetics goals? If you come to me with an aesthetics
focus, I don't need to do a peaking phase for you. Learn to move, build work
capacity, hypertrophy, reduce the reps and increase the load (a strength phase
benefits the aesthetics athlete — the stronger and more skilled at lifting, the
higher quality the hypertrophy sets). Then a programming strategy I love to use:
monthly undulating periodization, where from there we just continue to alternate
between our hypertrophy and strength phases forever. (Josh Lenartowicz, Australia's
best bodybuilder, an Olympia-level competitor, commented "that's just how I train,
love this.") So this is how some of the best do it. You can build muscle and lose
fat without following a specific periodized plan — keep working hard, target the
right muscles, apply the principles of 10 to 20 sets per muscle group per week,
take movements to a close proximity to fail, and eat well. Hope you enjoyed. See
you next time.
